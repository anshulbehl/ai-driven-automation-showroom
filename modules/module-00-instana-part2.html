Section 1.2 - Smart Alerts and Dual-Path Remediation

Estimated time: 30-40 minutes

Objective

In this section you will connect Instana to Ansible Automation Platform and exercise both remediation paths. You will create a webhook alert channel, an Application Perspective, and a Smart Alert, then break the Flask application and watch Event-Driven Ansible restore it automatically. Finally you will break it again with automation disarmed, and see which remediation Instana recommends for the incident and why.

By the end of this module you will have a working closed loop: Instana detects the outage, automation repairs it, and Instana confirms the recovery.

Learning Objectives

After completing this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify the Ansible job templates provisioned for your environment and verify their target host

  • Create a generic webhook alert channel that targets your Event-Driven Ansible listener

  • Create an Application Perspective and a service-level Smart Alert scoped to it

  • Trigger and observe fully automated remediation through Event-Driven Ansible

  • Read the NLP-ranked remediation recommendations Instana surfaces on a live incident

  • Explain when each path is the right choice

Prerequisites

Before starting, confirm:

  • You can log in to the Instana UI with the credentials from Section 1.1

  • You can log in to Ansible Automation Platform as {controller_username} / {controller_password} (click the AAP tab in the right-hand lab interface)

  • Your service lab-webapp-my-guid appears in the Instana Services tab and is receiving calls

Instana is shared with every other student in this workshop. Name the alert channel, Application Perspective, and Smart Alert you are about to create with your own my-guid, exactly as shown. Do not modify or delete objects belonging to other students, or any object that does not carry your own my-guid.

The lab provisioned six job templates for you in a dedicated Ansible Automation Platform organization. Look at them before wiring anything up, because their names and descriptions are what Instana matches against later.

  2. Navigate to Automation Execution → Templates

  3. Search for my-guid

You will see six templates, all in the Instana AIOps organization:

Notice also that the descriptions repeat phrases such as "anomalously low calls", "service is down", and "unreachable". That wording is deliberate, and Exercise 2 explains why.

Step 2: Confirm the Event-Driven Ansible Listener Is Running

Path 1 depends on a rulebook activation that is already running for you.

  1. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to Automation Decisions → Rulebook Activations

  2. Confirm that Instana AIOps shows status Running

What is a Rulebook Activation? A rulebook activation is a long-running listener managed by Event-Driven Ansible. It watches for incoming events — in this case, webhook payloads from Instana — and evaluates them against a set of rules. When an event matches a rule’s conditions, the activation automatically triggers the corresponding action, such as launching a Job Template. Think of it as an always-on "if this, then that" for your automation platform.

This activation runs the instana.yml rulebook, which listens for webhooks on port 5000. An OPEN issue launches remediation; a CLOSED issue records recovery without launching another job.

Your webhook URL is:

{eda_webhook_url}

The listener runs inside Ansible Automation Platform as a rulebook activation, not as a service you manage on the bastion. Use Automation Decisions → Rule Audit to inspect events that matched a rule and the resulting actions. The activation’s History tab shows activation runs and their output.

Step 4: Create the Application Perspective and Smart Alert

Instana does not let you attach a Smart Alert directly to a service. Smart Alerts are created in the context of an Application Perspective, so this is a two stage process:

  1. Create an Application Perspective containing your service

  2. Create a Smart Alert inside that Application Perspective

Stage 1: Create your Application Perspective

An Application Perspective is a scoped view of one or more services. Yours will contain a single service, lab-webapp-my-guid. In the wizard, select the model, filter to your service, exclude downstream services, name the perspective, then choose inbound calls.

  1. In the Instana UI, click Applications in the left navigation menu

  2. Click the blue + ADD button in the bottom-right corner of the screen and choose Add Application Perspective

  3. The wizard opens at Step 1: Select model — "What kind of application would you like to model?" Leave the default Services or endpoints selected and click Next

  4. On Step 2: Specify application, click Add filter, select service.name, and type lab-webapp-my-guid

    Type the full service name into the filter field — do not pick from the dropdown. The dropdown does not list every service in this shared instance, and lab-webapp-my-guid may not appear in it. Typing the complete name is what makes your service show up in the filter results.

  5. Confirm your service appears under Matched services in the last hour on the right side of the dialog

  6. Under Which downstream services would you like to include?, make sure to select No downstream services — it is not the default option

  7. Click Next

  8. On Step 3: Provide details, name the Application Perspective:

    lab-webapp-my-guid

  9. Under Are you interested just in the calls to this application, or also the internal calls?, leave Inbound calls selected

  10. Click Create to save the Application Perspective

Your filter must match only lab-webapp-my-guid. Every student’s Flask service is visible in this shared instance, and an Application Perspective scoped too broadly will alert on other students' services as well as your own.

Name the Application Perspective lab-webapp-my-guid so you can find it in the shared instance. For the Per service Smart Alert scope used below, the webhook’s issue.entityLabel is the affected service name, lab-webapp-my-guid. The rulebook strips lab-webapp- from that service name to find your job template. The perspective title itself does not determine the template name. The screenshot above uses an older sample title, wj8z2 Web App; follow the naming convention in these steps for your new perspective.

Allow a short time for the Application Perspective to populate with data before continuing.

The alert channel must be attached to the Smart Alert itself. The channel existing on its own does nothing. If you skip this, the alert will fire in the Instana UI but no webhook will reach Event-Driven Ansible, and Path 1 will appear to do nothing at all.

Advanced mode is required because the default Smart Alert mode uses an automatic baseline. You want a deterministic threshold here: fire when calls fall below one per minute. That makes the exercise reproducible within a workshop slot.

The saved alert may be displayed under a generic default title. That label is cosmetic. What matters is the configuration you entered. When it fires, the payload reports metricName: calls and the text "Number of calls is anomalously low".

Step 5: Confirm Your Actions Reached the Instana Action Catalog

Instana polls Ansible Automation Platform with a dedicated read and execute account and imports the job templates that account can see. Those imported templates become actions.

  1. In the Instana UI, click Automation in the left navigation menu

  2. Click Actions — this opens the Action Catalog

  3. Search for my-guid

You should find your six actions, each of type ANSIBLE.

Import is performed by the Instana backend polling Ansible Automation Platform on a schedule of roughly five minutes. If your actions are not listed yet, continue with the module and check again shortly.

The import is scoped by the credentials Instana uses, which only have access to the Instana AIOps organization. Instana cannot see or execute templates belonging to the rest of the lab.

Exercise 1: Path 1 - Automated Remediation

Break the service and let Event-Driven Ansible repair it without you.

Note your healthy baseline

  1. In Instana, navigate to Applications → Services → lab-webapp-my-guid

  2. Note the current Calls rate — a steady stream of requests from the traffic generator

  3. This is what healthy looks like. You will compare against it after the outage and again after recovery

Break the service

  1. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to Automation Execution → Templates

  2. Search for my-guid and open INSTANA | my-guid | Break Web App Service - Simulate low calls outage

  3. Click Launch

  4. The template prompts for a Limit. Enter:

    node2

  5. Click Launch to confirm

The limit is required. Your job template uses your lab inventory; node2 confines the play to your application host. Launching without it can target other hosts in your own inventory.

The job stops lab-webapp.service on node2. The Flask application stops answering, and the traffic generator on the bastion starts failing its health checks.

Watch the calls drop

  1. Switch to Instana, navigate to Applications → Services → lab-webapp-my-guid

  2. Compare the graph against the healthy baseline you noted at the start of this exercise

  3. Watch the Calls metric fall to zero within a few seconds

Wait for the Smart Alert to open

Allow roughly one to two minutes. The Smart Alert uses one minute evaluation windows, so it waits for a full minute below threshold before firing. This is what prevents a brief hiccup from triggering automation.

When it fires, the incident appears in Instana with state OPEN and the text "Number of calls is anomalously low".

If the alert has not fired after four or five minutes, do not keep waiting — this sometimes happens on the first attempt after a Smart Alert is created. Reset the cycle instead:

  1. Launch INSTANA | my-guid | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls from Ansible Automation Platform (with the limit node2) and let calls return

  2. Wait one to two minutes for the Calls metric to register steady traffic again

  3. Break the service a second time

The alert reliably fires on this second attempt once the metric has a fresh baseline to drop from. Then continue with the steps below as normal.

Confirm Event-Driven Ansible received the webhook

  1. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to Automation Decisions → Rulebook Activations

  2. Find Instana AIOps in the list and check that its fire count has increased. Both opening and closing alerts can match rules, so the count may rise again after recovery

  3. Navigate to Automation Decisions → Rule Audit and find the OPEN event for your lab-webapp-my-guid service. Open it to inspect the matched rule and action

The rulebook extracts my-guid from the entityLabel field of the payload and launches the matching restore template.

Watch the restore job run

  1. Navigate to Automation Execution → Jobs

  2. Find the latest INSTANA | my-guid | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls job launched by automation. It may already show Successful, or it may still be Running

  1. Open the job, inspect its output, and wait for Successful if it is still running

The job starts lab-webapp.service on node2 and verifies the health endpoint responds.

Confirm recovery in Instana

  1. Return to Instana, navigate to Applications → Services → lab-webapp-my-guid

  2. The Calls metric climbs back to its normal level as the traffic generator succeeds again

  3. After a few minutes above threshold — the 1 minute grace period plus evaluation time — the incident closes automatically and the state becomes CLOSED

The rulebook also matches CLOSED events and logs the recovery. Check Automation Decisions → Rule Audit for the recovery event.

You have completed Path 1. The sequence was:

  1. Service stops

  2. Instana detects the drop, alert opens after about one minute

  3. Webhook reaches the rulebook activation

  4. The activation launches your restore template

  5. The job restarts the service in about thirty seconds

  6. Instana observes recovery and closes the incident

No human took any action after the service failed.

Exercise 2: Path 2 - Operator-Driven Remediation from the Action Catalog

Now look at the same outage from the operator’s side: what Instana recommends, and why.

Disarm Path 1 first

Path 1 is still armed. If you break the service now, Event-Driven Ansible will repair it before you have a chance to act.

  1. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to Automation Decisions → Rulebook Activations

  2. Select Instana AIOps

  3. Disable the activation and wait for it to stop

Re-enable the Instana AIOps activation when you finish this exercise. Later sections assume it is running, and leaving it disabled will make the Section 1 behaviour impossible to reproduce.

Break the service again

Repeat the break step from Exercise 1: launch INSTANA | my-guid | Break Web App Service - Simulate low calls outage with the limit node2.

Wait for the Smart Alert to open in Instana as before. This time nothing repairs it, because the rulebook activation is disabled and cannot process the webhook.

Read the recommended action

  1. In Instana, open the incident for lab-webapp-my-guid

  2. Review the actions Instana recommends for this incident

Instana compares the incident text, "Number of calls is anomalously low", against the names and descriptions of the actions in its catalog, and ranks them by similarity. Your restore action is described as handling the case "when number of calls is anomalously low or zero …​ service is down, unreachable, or call rate has dropped significantly", so it scores as a relevant recommendation.

This matching is natural language processing built into Instana. It runs against the imported action metadata, with no external large language model involved, which is why it works in a disconnected on-premise deployment.

The quality of the recommendation depends entirely on how the Ansible job templates are described. The descriptions in this lab were written to contain the vocabulary that appears in the alert. Vague template descriptions produce weak recommendations, which is a practical argument for treating template metadata as part of your automation interface.

Open the recommended action

  1. Select INSTANA | my-guid | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls

  2. Review its details: the action type is ANSIBLE, and it maps to the job template you inspected in Step 1

This is the same job template that Event-Driven Ansible launched for you in Exercise 1. Instana is not holding a copy of the automation. It holds a reference to your job template and calls the Ansible Automation Platform API to run it.

Run the action from Instana

You can execute the action directly from Instana — this is what a production operator would do at incident time, with the run attributed to them. Since every student environment publishes its actions to the same shared catalog, take care before you run anything.

  1. Select INSTANA | my-guid | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls and click Run

  2. In the run prompt, type node2 into the host limit field and press Enter so it becomes its own entry

  3. Run the action and confirm the job starts in Ansible Automation Platform

Only run actions that carry your own guid. The shared Action Catalog contains the actions of every student environment — an INSTANA | <other guid> | …​ action launches a job template in another student’s environment. Check the name before you click Run.

Press Enter after typing the host limit. The limit only takes effect once it is committed as its own entry in the field. If you type node2 and move on without pressing Enter, the limit is silently ignored and the job template can run on all hosts in your own lab inventory. Check that the node2 entry appears before running the action.

Restore the service

If you would rather restore from Ansible Automation Platform, this runs the same job template that the Instana action points at.

  1. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to Automation Execution → Templates

  2. Open INSTANA | my-guid | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls

  3. Click Launch, enter the limit node2, and confirm

  4. Wait for the job to complete, then return to Instana and confirm calls resume and the incident closes

Re-enable Path 1

  1. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to Automation Decisions → Rulebook Activations

  2. Enable the Instana AIOps activation

  3. Confirm it returns to Running

Use Path 1 where the failure mode is understood and the fix is safe to apply unattended. Use Path 2 where the correct response depends on context a rule cannot capture, or where your change process requires a person to decide.

Most organisations run both. The value of the Action Catalog is that the operator making the judgement call is handed a short, ranked list of validated automation instead of a blank terminal.

Taking It Further: AI-Driven Remediation

In Path 1, Event-Driven Ansible remediated the incident directly: the rulebook matched the alert and launched one job template, pre-selected by you when you wrote the rule. The decision was made in advance.

The next step is to let the automation itself choose the remediation. Your job templates are already in Ansible Automation Platform and your actions are already in the Instana catalog — so you can bake AI into the playbooks and build an AI router and dispatch pattern:

  • an AI router job receives the Smart Alert payload and asks a large language model to pick the most likely remediation from your complete automation catalog, not from one hardcoded mapping — the payload already carries the incident text, the affected entity in entityLabel, and Instana’s own remediation hint in issue.suggestion

  • an approval gate pauses the workflow so a human reviews the AI’s choice before anything executes

  • a dispatcher launches the approved job template against the affected host

The pattern in playbooks

The pattern is three nodes in an Ansible Automation Platform workflow:

Node 1

Node 2

Node 3

ai_route_remediation.yml — AI picks the template

Workflow approval node — you review and approve

dispatch_remediation.yml — launches the approved template

and the approval gate feeding the dispatcher:

The snippets below are from a working demo of this pattern against Instana alerts. The AI router fetches your job template catalog from the AAP API and asks the LLM to pick one:

- name: Fetch job template catalog from AAP
  ansible.builtin.uri:
    url: "https://{{ controller_host }}/api/controller/v2/job_templates/?page_size=100"
    method: GET
    headers:
      Authorization: "Bearer {{ controller_token }}"
    status_code: 200
  register: jt_response

- name: Build LLM prompt
  ansible.builtin.set_fact:
    llm_prompt: |
      You are an AIOps routing agent. An observability alert has fired and you must
      select the single best remediation action from the job template catalog below.

      ALERT CONTEXT:
      - Text: {{ alert_text }}
      - Severity: {{ alert_severity }}
      - Entity Label: {{ alert_entity_label }}
      {% if alert_suggestion | length > 0 %}
      - Instana Suggestion: {{ alert_suggestion }}
      {% endif %}

      JOB TEMPLATE CATALOG (from Ansible Automation Platform):
      {{ template_catalog }}

      INSTRUCTIONS:
      - Select exactly ONE job template that best remediates this alert
      - If confidence is below 70% or nothing clearly matches, set template to "ESCALATE"
      - Respond with ONLY a JSON object:
      {"template": "<exact template name>", "confidence": <0-100>, "reasoning": "<one sentence>"}

The alert context comes straight from the Instana webhook payload you examined in Part 1 — alert_entity_label is the payload’s entityLabel, and alert_suggestion is issue.suggestion. Anything Instana already tells you about the incident is free routing signal.

The router then records its decision and hands control to the workflow:

- name: Pass routing decision to workflow via set_stats
  ansible.builtin.set_stats:
    data:
      selected_template: "{{ routing_decision.template }}"
      routing_confidence: "{{ routing_decision.confidence }}"
      routing_reasoning: "{{ routing_decision.reasoning }}"
      alert_fqdn: "{{ alert_fqdn }}"
      alert_entity_label: "{{ alert_entity_label }}"

- name: Fail the node if AI says ESCALATE
  ansible.builtin.fail:
    msg: >-
      AI router selected ESCALATE (confidence: {{ routing_decision.confidence }}%).
      Alert '{{ alert_text }}' does not match any known remediation.
      Sending to on-call team for manual investigation.
  when: routing_decision.template == "ESCALATE"

set_stats is how the three nodes share state: the approval node pauses the workflow for a human decision, and the dispatcher reads the same stats. Failing the node on ESCALATE routes low-confidence incidents to a person instead of guessing.

Finally, the dispatcher launches the approved template against the affected host:

- name: Find job template by name in AAP
  ansible.builtin.uri:
    url: "https://{{ controller_host }}/api/controller/v2/job_templates/?name={{ selected_template | urlencode }}"
    method: GET
    headers:
      Authorization: "Bearer {{ controller_token }}"
    status_code: 200
  register: template_lookup

- name: Launch remediation job template
  ansible.builtin.uri:
    url: "https://{{ controller_host }}/api/controller/v2/job_templates/{{ template_lookup.json.results[0].id }}/launch/"
    method: POST
    headers:
      Authorization: "Bearer {{ controller_token }}"
    body_format: json
    body:
      extra_vars:
        target_host: "{{ alert_fqdn }}"
        entity_label: "{{ alert_entity_label }}"
        routed_by: "ai"
        routing_confidence: "{{ routing_confidence }}"
    status_code: [200, 201]
  register: launched_job

Notice the dispatcher never contains remediation logic itself — it looks up whatever template the AI selected and launches it. That is what makes the pattern catalog-driven: add a new remediation job template to AAP and the router can pick it immediately, with no playbook changes.

The dividing line to keep in mind: rules for known failure modes, AI for everything else. Path 1 is faster and fully predictable, but only covers what you wrote a rule for. The AI-routed pattern trades a little latency for coverage of incidents nobody anticipated — and the approval gate is what keeps it governed: every AI decision is reviewed, attributable, and auditable before it touches a host.

Summary

You have:

  • Reviewed the job templates named for your my-guid and checked the launch limit for your application host

  • Created a generic webhook alert channel pointing at your rulebook activation

  • Created an Application Perspective and a Smart Alert scoped to your own service

  • Watched Event-Driven Ansible detect and repair an outage with no human involvement

  • Read the remediation Instana recommended against a live incident, and seen that it points at your own job template

  • Compared the two paths on speed, attribution, and flexibility

The wider point: observability is not only about seeing problems. Once an observability platform can both describe an incident and reach your automation, detection and repair become one loop rather than two disconnected activities.

The AI router and dispatch pattern is the natural next step on that path: detection and repair become one loop, with AI choosing the fix and a human approving it before it runs.
